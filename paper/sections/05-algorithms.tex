\section{Algorithms}
\label{sec:algorithms}

Three algorithms govern the prototype's higher-level behaviour: a
tiled multi-QR recognition routine that recovers several stickers
from a single camera capture (Section~\ref{sec:algo-qr}); a
pixel-level RGB classifier that estimates banana surface spotting
percentage (Section~\ref{sec:algo-banana}); and a per-product risk
score that combines all sensing channels into a single 0--100
indicator (Section~\ref{sec:algo-risk}).

\subsection{Tiled Multi-QR Recognition}
\label{sec:algo-qr}

A single-pass QR decoder applied to a typical ESP32-CAM capture
returns at most one symbol per frame because most open-source
decoders terminate on first success. In a cabinet setting several
items can be visible simultaneously, so registering them one at a
time defeats the purpose of automatic identification.

The proposed multi-QR routine operates on a single camera capture.
The input frame is downscaled to 480\,px on its longer edge to bound
per-pass runtime. A 3\,$\times$\,3 grid of overlapping windows is
produced; each window covers approximately 45\,\% of the frame width
and height with a 25\,\% overlap between neighbours. Under this
geometry any QR symbol that fits in a 45\,\% region is fully
contained in at least one window. Each window is passed independently
to a portable QR decoder (\texttt{zxing2} on the web target and
\texttt{qr\_code\_scanner} on the Android target), and decoded
payloads are deduplicated by parsed JSON \texttt{productId} into a
session set, so that a sticker present in two overlapping windows is
registered exactly once.

End-to-end runtime remains under 200\,ms per frame on a midrange
Android device. The routine trades a small amount of redundant
decoding work for substantially higher coverage of multi-product
scenes.

\subsection{Pixel-Level RGB Ripeness Estimation}
\label{sec:algo-banana}

Visible browning is a strong qualitative indicator of late-stage
banana ripeness. Rather than training a convolutional model, the
prototype performs a transparent pixel-level RGB analysis that runs
in a few milliseconds on commodity hardware and yields a single
percentage.

Given a JPEG capture $I$ in the sRGB colour space, the classifier
first partitions pixels into four classes:

\begin{align*}
\mathcal{B}_{\text{yellow}} &= \{(r, g, b) : r > 150 \land g > 130 \land b < 110\}, \\
\mathcal{B}_{\text{brown}}  &= \{(r, g, b) : 100 \leq r \leq 200 \\
                            & \quad \land 50 \leq g \leq 130 \land b < 90\}, \\
\mathcal{B}_{\text{dark}}   &= \{(r, g, b) : r < 90 \land g < 80 \land b < 70\}, \\
\mathcal{B}_{\text{bg}}     &= \{(r, g, b) : r > 220 \land g > 220 \land b > 220\}.
\end{align*}

Each pixel of $I$ is assigned to whichever class contains it. Let
$n_y$, $n_b$, $n_d$ denote pixel counts for yellow, brown, and dark
respectively. The reported spot coverage is

\begin{equation}
\sigma = \frac{n_b + n_d}{\max(n_y + n_b + n_d, \, 1)} \cdot 100\,\%,
\label{eq:sigma}
\end{equation}

and the reported ripeness stage is

\begin{equation}
\text{stage}(\sigma) =
\begin{cases}
\text{Fresh}     & \text{if } \sigma <  10\,\% \\
\text{Spotting}  & \text{if } 10\,\% \leq \sigma < 40\,\% \\
\text{Spoiling}  & \text{if } 40\,\% \leq \sigma < 70\,\% \\
\text{Spoiled}   & \text{if } \sigma \geq 70\,\%.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:stage}
\end{equation}

The classifier maintains no state across frames; each capture is
processed independently, which yields outputs that are reproducible
from the captured image alone. A representative output of
$\sigma = 30.6\,\%$, labelled \emph{Spotting}, is shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:app}(b).

\subsection{Per-Product Risk Score}
\label{sec:algo-risk}

A per-product risk score $R \in [0, 100]$ combines six channels:
expiration time remaining, ambient temperature, ambient humidity,
gas trend, visible spotting, and weight-change rate. The score is
capped at 100 and partitioned into three display bands: Fresh
($R < 40$), Consume Soon ($40 \leq R < 70$), and Spoilage Risk
($R \geq 70$).

For a product $p$, the score is computed as

\begin{equation}
R(p) = \min\!\bigl(100,\, r_{\text{exp}}(p) + r_T + r_H + r_G + r_V(p) + r_W(p)\bigr),
\label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}

where each contribution is bounded by a per-channel maximum and
rounded down to the nearest integer:

\begin{itemize}
  \item $r_{\text{exp}}$ is zero when expiration is more than a week
        away, ramps linearly to 40 as the expiration date is reached,
        and remains at 40 thereafter.
  \item $r_T = \max(0, T - 8^\circ\mathrm{C}) \cdot 1.5$, capped at 20.
  \item $r_H = \max(0, H - 70\,\%) \cdot 0.5$, capped at 10.
  \item $r_G = \max(0, G - G_{\text{baseline}}) \cdot 0.4$, capped at 10,
        where $G_{\text{baseline}}$ is the cabinet's recalibration
        baseline.
  \item $r_V = \sigma / 5$, capped at 10, applied only to products
        whose category supports visual analysis.
  \item $r_W = 5$ if the measured weight has dropped by more than
        50\,\% since registration, otherwise zero.
\end{itemize}

Each per-channel cap is intentionally lower than 100 so that no
single sensor channel can push a product into the Spoilage Risk
band; the band is reached only when several channels coincide. This
multi-channel requirement matches the observation that food
deterioration typically manifests across multiple indicators
simultaneously.

The score is recomputed inside the Flutter application each time a
sensor reading arrives. The alert engine reads the current score on
each refresh, which avoids the need to persist scoring state across
application restarts.
