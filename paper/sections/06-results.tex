\section{Demonstration Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section presents qualitative results obtained during
demonstration sessions of the assembled prototype. The reported
sessions used the calibrated load-cell subsystem against a known
reference mass, the DHT11 and MQ135 sensors in ambient indoor
conditions, the ESP32-CAM running the dual-port HTTP servers, and the
Flutter web build polling the bridge once per second. We emphasize
that the results below are from prototype-level demonstrations and not
from controlled long-duration deployments; the system was designed
explicitly as a reproducible reference design, and the discussion in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} documents the limitations honestly.

\subsection{Weight Subsystem Calibration and Performance}

After deriving a calibration factor of $20.626$ from a single-purpose
calibration sketch using a reference 1~kg test mass, the integrated
load-cell subsystem was exercised across several known masses inside
the cabinet. With the cabinet stationary on a level surface, repeated
measurements of a 10~kg reference load remained between 9.8~kg and
10.0~kg, with the small residual variation attributable to platform
flexure under the load cells and to load-cell creep.

Two further qualitative observations were repeatedly confirmed:
(i)~product placement and removal were detected reliably for
products with a stable bearing surface and a mass of roughly 50~g or
greater, and (ii)~very light products (e.g.\ a folded piece of paper)
were not reliably detected, because their signal was within the noise
band produced by ambient vibration. A representative reading near the
detection threshold was 0.97~g (Listing~\ref{lst:bridgelog}), which
reflects the system correctly observing a small perturbation that
does not correspond to a registered product.

\subsection{Environmental Monitoring}

During an indoor demonstration session, the DHT11 reported between
27.4~$^{\circ}$C and 27.8~$^{\circ}$C and between 61\% and 63\%
relative humidity over the course of approximately ten minutes
without active climate control. The MQ135 reported analog values in
the 41--46 range over the same window, with brief excursions toward
46 when a hand was held close to the sensor. These observations
confirm the firmware's basic responsiveness to changes in the
cabinet's air composition, while supporting our position that the
MQ135 channel should be treated as a \emph{trend indicator} rather
than a calibrated gas-concentration measurement
(Section~\ref{sec:impl-fw}).

\subsection{Mobile Application Behavior}

Figure~\ref{fig:app} captures the application during a representative
demonstration session. The Dashboard (panel~a) renders the four
sensor cards, the registered banana product, and a summary of the
two active alerts. The Camera screen (panel~b) overlays the live MJPEG
stream from the ESP32-CAM with the output of the in-frame banana
classifier, which on the captured frame reported
$\sigma = 30.6\%$ spot coverage, labeled \emph{Spotting -- light
spotting, still consumable}. The Products screen (panel~c) renders
the registered banana with a three-day-until-expiration status band
and the corresponding \emph{Expiring Soon} chip; the Alerts screen
(panel~d) aggregates the two automatically generated alerts (one for
the upcoming expiration and one indicating the offline sensor board);
and the Settings screen (panel~e) shows the
\texttt{http://localhost:8787} bridge URL configuration and the
\emph{Not configured} Firebase status that places the application in
its intended in-memory fallback mode.

\subsection{Bridge and End-to-End Operation}

Listing~\ref{lst:bridgelog} reproduces a short window of bridge log
output observed during the session. Each \texttt{[serial]} line
corresponds to one decoded JSON record produced by the Arduino Uno
and translated into the bridge's internal state record; the
\texttt{[http]} startup line confirms that the bridge is exposing
its endpoint on the expected port. During the same window, the
mobile application polled \texttt{GET /sensors} once per second and
the bridge returned a representative record of
\begin{lstlisting}
{"temperature":27.7,"humidity":63.0,
 "gasValue":42,"weight":0,
 "updatedAt":1781692341,"online":true}
\end{lstlisting}
which the Dashboard rendered without any intervening cache, confirming
the end-to-end path Arduino~$\to$~bridge~$\to$~mobile application
during operation.

\subsection{Multi-QR Decoding}

The tiled multi-QR decoder was exercised on printed A4 sheets of
QR stickers placed at varied positions and orientations within the
ESP32-CAM's field of view. Up to four distinct QR codes were recovered
from a single capture under cabinet illumination, with the per-frame
runtime remaining under 200~ms on a midrange Android device. The
practical takeaway is that the multi-QR pipeline removes the need for
the user to scan stickers individually, which is the workflow
penalty that motivates this work
(Section~\ref{sec:algo-qr}).

\subsection{Visual Spoilage Estimation}

The pixel-level banana classifier produced consistent, reproducible
spot-coverage percentages across captures of the same banana taken
within seconds of each other. As bananas progressed from yellow toward
heavier surface spotting over a multi-day window, the reported
percentage increased monotonically, with the reported stage label
transitioning from \emph{Fresh} to \emph{Spotting} and (in extended
informal observation) eventually toward \emph{Spoiling}. The classifier
remains intentionally transparent: the same input image will always
produce the same output, which makes its behavior auditable and
suitable as a baseline against which future model-based classifiers
can be compared.
